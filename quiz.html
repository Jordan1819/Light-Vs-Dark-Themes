In controlled experiments on decision-making, researchers often observe that changing the default option—what happens if a participant does nothing—can produce large shifts in behavior without altering incentives or information. This phenomenon is frequently called the “power of defaults.” While defaults are sometimes dismissed as mere presentation, they can change how people interpret effort, risk, and social expectations, thereby shaping choices.

Consider two identical enrollment forms for a voluntary data-sharing program. The first is opt-in, requiring a box to be checked; the second is opt-out, requiring a box to be unchecked. Although participants face the same options and consequences, opt-out systems generally see far higher enrollment. One explanation emphasizes cognitive frictions: the minimal effort to change the default can loom large when attention is scarce. Another explanation emphasizes implied endorsement: a default may be read as the designer's recommendation, nudging participants to comply.

Yet defaults do not operate in a vacuum. Their influence depends on context, notably the perceived reversibility of choices and the salience of trade-offs. Defaults are weaker when changing one's mind is easy and costless, and stronger when switching seems complex or socially conspicuous. Moreover, defaults can backfire. If users sense manipulation, they may scrutinize the choice more carefully and reject the default on principle, a reaction sometimes labeled reactance.

Policy designers therefore face a calibration problem. Defaults can promote beneficial behaviors—such as retirement saving or privacy protection—without mandating them, but only if they are transparent and aligned with participant welfare. When defaults are opaque or self-serving, short-run gains may come at the expense of trust, eroding long-run effectiveness. The challenge is not to eliminate defaults—they are unavoidable in any interface—but to choose them so that inattention does not become exploitation.
